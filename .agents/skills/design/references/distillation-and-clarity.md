# Distillation and Content Clarity

Principles for editing, simplifying, and clarifying technical content.

## 1. Ruthless Distillation

- The Essence Test: Every slide must have exactly ONE core takeaway. If a slide presents multiple unrelated findings, split it into multiple slides.
- Text Budget: Aim for ~40 words of prose per slide. If text overflows or forces font shrinkage below 20pt, cut copy rather than shrinking font size.
- Cut and Refine: Write the initial draft, cut the word count in half, and eliminate all conversational filler, hedges, and redundant phrases.
- The 4-Item Rule: Limit competing visual elements or bullet items to at most 4 per slide to prevent cognitive overload.

## 2. Action Titles vs. Topic Labels

Transform passive topic labels into active assertions that state the conclusion:

- Passive: LSTM Gating Functions
- Active: Forget gates allow LSTMs to reset obsolete state across long sequences
- Passive: BLEU Score Comparison
- Active: Deep 4-layer LSTMs outperform phrase-based SMT baselines by 0.5 BLEU
- Passive: Source Sequence Reversal
- Active: Reversing source words reduces initial time lag and simplifies backpropagation

## 3. Copy Cadence and Anti-Slop Rules

- Editorial Authority: Speak with confident taste and technical precision. Eliminate hedging verbs and adverbs (might, could consider, perhaps, arguably, essentially). Pick a direction and commit.
- Eliminate Marketing Buzzwords: Remove generic SaaS/AI filler words (streamline, empower, supercharge, cutting-edge, next-generation, seamless, transformative). Name specific mathematical mechanisms and empirical gains.
- Avoid Aphoristic Cadence: Do not repeat manufactured-contrast slogans across sections ("X. No Y.", "Not a feature. A platform."). State factual assertions directly.
- Em-Dash Restraint: Avoid em-dash saturation. Use commas, colons, periods, or parentheses.
- Say it Once: If the slide heading conveys the conclusion, do not repeat the exact same sentence in the first bullet point.
- Single Concept per Bullet: Keep bullet points short, telegraphic, and focused on one empirical or mathematical observation.
